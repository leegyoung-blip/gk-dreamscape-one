DREAMSCAPE ONE · Teaching Explanation Refinement

Six offline, human-refined Mathematics teaching examples based on one reviewer’s notes. Visual, step-by-step and written for Primary pupils.

REFERENCE MATERIAL ONLY. These are not NOVA-generated answers, production teaching content, curriculum approval or proof that pupils will understand them. Q2 is a new, unreviewed question proposal.

Primary 3 · Case 1

SPACING VALID MAIN

The markers are equally spaced 6 m apart on a straight path. Find the distance from marker 4 to marker 12.

  1. Look at marker 4 and marker 12 on the number line. We need the distance between them.
  2. Count the gaps, not the markers. There are 12 − 4 = 8 gaps.
    12 − 4 = 8 gaps
  3. Each gap is 6 m. Multiply 8 by 6 to find the distance.
    8 × 6 = 48 m

Answer: 48 m

Quick check: From marker 4 to marker 5 is one gap. That helps us count correctly.

A mistake to watch for: Nine markers are shown, but there are only eight gaps between them.

Teacher review note: Keep Variant B's clear number line; remove the unnecessary fourth main step. Keep a short optional check.

Primary 3 · Case 2

SPACING VALID ADJACENT

Markers are placed 9 m apart along a straight path. Ella walks from marker 2 to marker 7, then turns back and stops at marker 4. How far does Ella walk altogether?

NEW multistep replacement proposed. The original one-gap question is kept in the JSON; it has not been re-reviewed.

  1. Follow Ella from marker 2 to marker 7. Count 7 − 2 = 5 gaps.
    7 − 2 = 5 gaps
  2. She turns back from marker 7 to marker 4. Count 7 − 4 = 3 more gaps.
    7 − 4 = 3 gaps
  3. She walks 5 + 3 = 8 gaps altogether. Each gap is 9 m.
    5 + 3 = 8 gaps
  4. Multiply to find the total distance she walked.
    8 × 9 = 72 m

Answer: 72 m

Quick check: Ella walked past marker 4 and came back, so her walk is longer than the straight distance from marker 2 to marker 4.

A mistake to watch for: Do not count only from marker 2 to marker 4. Ella also walked to marker 7 before turning back.

Teacher review note: Original Q2 was too easy. Retain original as a one-gap warm-up; propose this NEW multistep question for later review. The original A-versus-B judgment does not score this replacement.

Primary 5 · Case 3

CHAIN VALID HALF

Bella has three times as many objects as Alan. Chen has half as many as Bella. Together they have 66. How many does Chen have?

  1. Start with Alan. Bella has three times as many as Alan. Chen has half as many as Bella.
  2. Draw Alan with 2 equal parts, Bella with 6, and Chen with 3. All the small parts are the same size.
    Alan 2 parts; Bella 6 parts; Chen 3 parts
  3. There are 2 + 6 + 3 = 11 equal parts altogether. The total is 66 objects.
    66 ÷ 11 = 6 objects in one part
  4. Chen has 3 parts. Multiply to find Chen's number of objects.
    3 × 6 = 18 objects
  5. Check: Alan has 12, Bella has 36, and Chen has 18. The total is 66.
    12 + 36 + 18 = 66

Answer: 18 objects

Quick check: Chen has half of Bella's 36 objects, which is 18.

A mistake to watch for: The question says Chen has half of Bella's objects, not half of Alan's objects.

Teacher review note: Prefer Variant B and the aligned 2:6:3 bar model. Do not lead with x and fraction equations.

Primary 5 · Case 4

CHAIN VALID SHARED BASE

Bella has twice as many objects as Alan; Chen has three times as many as Alan. Together they have 72. How many does Chen have?

  1. Both comparisons are about Alan. Draw Alan as one equal part.
    Alan 1 part
  2. Bella has 2 parts and Chen has 3 parts. All the parts are the same size.
    1 + 2 + 3 = 6 parts
  3. All 6 parts add up to 72 objects. Divide to find one part.
    72 ÷ 6 = 12 objects
  4. Chen has 3 parts. Find how many objects Chen has.
    3 × 12 = 36 objects
  5. Check: 12 + 24 + 36 = 72. Bella has twice Alan's number, and Chen has three times Alan's number.
    12 + 24 + 36 = 72

Answer: 36 objects

Quick check: Look at the bars again. Chen's bar is three times Alan's bar.

A mistake to watch for: Do not make Chen three times Bella. Both Bella and Chen are compared with Alan.

Teacher review note: Prefer Variant A and the shared-base bar model. Avoid an unnecessary dependency table.

Primary 6 · Case 5

RATIO VALID MAIN

The ratio of quantities A:B was 3:2. A increases by 6 and B increases by 24; the new ratio becomes 1:2. Find the original amount of B.

  1. Before the increase, A has 3 equal parts and B has 2. Let u stand for one equal part before the increase.
    Before: A = 3u, B = 2u
  2. A increases by 6 and B increases by 24. After the increase, B is twice A.
    After: A = 3u + 6, B = 2u + 24
  3. Draw two copies of A after the increase. Together, they have 6 equal parts and 12 more. That is the same as B after the increase.
    6u + 12 = 2u + 24
  4. Remove 2 equal parts and 12 from both sides. Then 4 equal parts make 12, so one part is 3.
    4u = 12, so u = 3
  5. B originally had 2 parts, so B was 6. Check: A becomes 15 and B becomes 30. B is twice A.
    B = 2 × 3 = 6; 30 = 2 × 15

Answer: 6

Quick check: Before, A:B is 9:6, which is 3:2. After, A:B is 15:30, which is 1:2.

A mistake to watch for: The size of one part before the increase is not the same as the size of one part in the new ratio.

Teacher review note: Prefer A's bar approach, but make the bar model visible and explain u in everyday language. Algebra supports the bars instead of replacing them.

Primary 6 · Case 6

RATIO VALID DIFFERENT SCALE

Quantities A:B start in the ratio 2:3. A increases by 5 and B increases by 16, giving a new ratio 1:2. Find original B.

  1. Before the increase, A has 2 equal parts and B has 3. Let u stand for one equal part before the increase.
    Before: A = 2u, B = 3u
  2. A gains 5 and B gains 16. After the increase, B has twice as much as A.
    After: A = 2u + 5, B = 3u + 16
  3. Make two copies of A after the increase. They add up to 4 equal parts and 10 more. This equals B after the increase.
    4u + 10 = 3u + 16
  4. Remove 3 equal parts and 10 from both sides. One equal part is worth 6.
    u = 6
  5. B originally had 3 parts, so B was 18. Check: A becomes 17 and B becomes 34. B is twice A.
    B = 3 × 6 = 18; 34 = 2 × 17

Answer: 18

Quick check: Before, A:B is 12:18, which is 2:3. After, A:B is 17:34, which is 1:2.

A mistake to watch for: u means one equal part before the increase. We must not treat the new ratio's parts as the same size.

Teacher review note: Prefer A's explanation but replace technical phrases with clear definitions, and add the missing bar model.
