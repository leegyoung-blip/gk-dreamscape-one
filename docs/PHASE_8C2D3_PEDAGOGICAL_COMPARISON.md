## Blind comparison cards

### 1. SPACING_VALID_MAIN — P3

**Question:** The markers are equally spaced 6 m apart on a straight path. Find the distance from marker 4 to marker 12.

**Correct answer for both:** 48 m

#### Variant A

1. The target is the distance from the 4th marker to the 12th marker.
2. To find that distance, we need the number of 6 m intervals between these marker positions.
3. The interval count is 12 − 4 = 8. Each interval is 6 m.
4. Multiply eight intervals by 6 m: 8 × 6 = 48 m.

**Representation:** table — Target: distance; prerequisite: interval count; given: 6 m per interval

**Misconception response:** Ask why subtracting indices gives gaps and check adjacent marker positions.

#### Variant B

1. Put a small mark for the 4th marker and another for the 12th. Each neighbouring pair has a 6 m gap.
2. The marker numbers tell us the positions, not the number of gaps. Subtract 4 from 12 to count 8 gaps.
3. All 8 gaps are 6 m long. Multiply 8 by 6 to get the whole distance of 48 m.
4. Between the 4th and 5th marker there is only one gap, even though two markers are shown. That gap is 6 m.

**Representation:** number_line — Label the 4th and 12th markers, then show eight identical gaps of 6 m between them.

**Misconception response:** Mark two neighbouring positions and point to the one gap; intervals equal the difference in position numbers.

**Comparison review questions:** Which version makes the central concept visible? Does the extra representation prevent a likely error, or add unnecessary cognitive load? Is the alternative still mathematically sound for the same learner?

### 2. SPACING_VALID_ADJACENT — P3

**Question:** The markers are equally spaced 9 m apart. Find the distance from the 1st marker to the 2nd.

**Correct answer for both:** 9 m

#### Variant A

1. Mark the 1st and 2nd markers side by side. There is one equal gap of 9 m between them.
2. Subtract the first position number from the second: 2 minus 1 gives one gap, not two gaps.
3. One gap is 9 m long, so the total distance is 1 times 9, which is 9 m.
4. Point to the single space between the two markers. We count spaces, not the number of markers.

**Representation:** number_line — Draw two labelled marks with one 9 m gap between them.

**Misconception response:** Trace the space separating the two markers; there is exactly one interval.

#### Variant B

1. Find the number of gaps first, then multiply by the distance in one gap.
2. The endpoints are positions 1 and 2. Find their difference.
3. 2 − 1 = 1 gap, and one gap is 9 m.
4. 1 × 9 = 9 m; this is the distance asked for.

**Representation:** table — Position difference before distance calculation

**Misconception response:** An adjacent pair encloses exactly one space even though two markers are pictured.

**Comparison review questions:** Which version makes the central concept visible? Does the extra representation prevent a likely error, or add unnecessary cognitive load? Is the alternative still mathematically sound for the same learner?

### 3. CHAIN_VALID_HALF — P5

**Question:** Bella has three times as many objects as Alan. Chen has half as many as Bella. Together they have 66. How many does Chen have?

**Correct answer for both:** 18 objects

#### Variant A

1. The question asks for Chen’s objects, not for Bella’s.
2. Chen has half of Bella. To get Chen we need Bella, which is three times Alan.
3. If Alan has x objects, Bella has 3x and Chen has 3x/2. Their sum is 66.
4. x + 3x + 3x/2 = 66, so 11x/2 = 66, x = 12. Chen has (3 × 12)/2 = 18 objects.

**Representation:** table — Named quantities and their expressions relative to Alan

**Misconception response:** Keep the relationship Chen = Bella/2; do not compute Chen = Alan/2.

#### Variant B

1. Use Alan as the reference. Bella has three Alan-sized amounts; Chen has half of Bella, not half of Alan.
2. To avoid half-bars, represent Alan with 2 little equal parts, Bella with 6, and Chen with 3. All bars use the same-sized part.
3. There are 2 plus 6 plus 3, or 11 equal parts altogether. Divide 66 by 11 to find 6 objects in one part.
4. Chen has 3 equal parts. Multiply 3 by 6 to find that Chen has 18 objects.
5. Check: Alan has 12, Bella has 36, Chen has 18; Bella is three times Alan, Chen is half Bella, and 12 + 36 + 18 = 66.

**Representation:** bar_model — Aligned bars contain 2, 6 and 3 equally sized sections respectively. The same section size is used for everyone.

**Misconception response:** Label each relationship first and split all bars into one common small part.

**Comparison review questions:** Which version makes the central concept visible? Does the extra representation prevent a likely error, or add unnecessary cognitive load? Is the alternative still mathematically sound for the same learner?

### 4. CHAIN_VALID_SHARED_BASE — P5

**Question:** Bella has twice as many objects as Alan; Chen has three times as many as Alan. Together they have 72. How many does Chen have?

**Correct answer for both:** 36 objects

#### Variant A

1. Both comparisons refer to Alan, so Alan is one reference part in our bar model.
2. Show Alan as 1 equal part, Bella as 2 parts and Chen as 3 parts. All parts have the same value.
3. Add 1 plus 2 plus 3 to make 6 equal parts. Divide the total of 72 by 6, giving 12 per part.
4. Chen owns 3 parts, so multiply 3 by 12 and obtain 36 objects for Chen.
5. Check Alan 12, Bella 24 and Chen 36. Bella is twice Alan, Chen is three times Alan, and 12 + 24 + 36 = 72.

**Representation:** bar_model — Place three bars of 1, 2 and 3 common parts in alignment.

**Misconception response:** Underline the named reference in each statement; both comparisons refer to Alan.

#### Variant B

1. Chen depends on the one-reference amount, which depends on the total of six parts.
2. Bella is twice Alan and Chen three times Alan: 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 identical Alan amounts.
3. 72 ÷ 6 = 12, so Alan = 12 and Bella = 24.
4. Chen = 3 × 12 = 36; 12 + 24 + 36 = 72.

**Representation:** table — Dependency table with base amount, multiplied amounts and total

**Misconception response:** Explicitly label that both multipliers refer to Alan, not Bella.

**Comparison review questions:** Which version makes the central concept visible? Does the extra representation prevent a likely error, or add unnecessary cognitive load? Is the alternative still mathematically sound for the same learner?

### 5. RATIO_VALID_MAIN — P6

**Question:** The ratio of quantities A:B was 3:2. A increases by 6 and B increases by 24; the new ratio becomes 1:2. Find the original amount of B.

**Correct answer for both:** 6 objects

#### Variant A

1. In both ratios A is the first term and B is the second term.
2. Before, A has 3u and B 2u. After, A has v and B 2v. Draw the equal-unit bars separately by state.
3. From +6 and +24, 3u + 6 = v and 2u + 24 = 2v.
4. Substitute: 2u + 24 = 2(3u + 6). Therefore u = 3, so original B = 2u = 6 objects.

**Representation:** bar_model — Two independently scaled ratio bar pairs linked using the stated additions

**Misconception response:** Do not equate u and v; they represent different unit sizes for separate ratio states.

#### Variant B

1. Keep A first and B second in both ratios. The original ratio is 3 to 2; both A and B increase by the stated amounts.
2. Use a before-state unit u: original A = 3u and original B = 2u. Those before-state units are the same size.
3. After the additions, the quantities become A = 3u + 6 and B = 2u + 24. Neither quantity stays unchanged.
4. The new ratio 1:2 means B is twice A after the changes: 2(3u + 6) = 2u + 24. This gives u = 3.
5. Before: A = 9 and B = 6. After: A = 15 and B = 30. Their ratios are 3:2 and 1:2, and both additions are correct.

**Representation:** ratio_table — Two rows for before and after, with A and B columns and a separate change row. Do not equate the before and after ratio units.

**Misconception response:** Use separate before/after rows and check the differences in actual quantities.

**Comparison review questions:** Which version makes the central concept visible? Does the extra representation prevent a likely error, or add unnecessary cognitive load? Is the alternative still mathematically sound for the same learner?

### 6. RATIO_VALID_DIFFERENT_SCALE — P6

**Question:** Quantities A:B start in the ratio 2:3. A increases by 5 and B increases by 16, giving a new ratio 1:2. Find original B.

**Correct answer for both:** 18 objects

#### Variant A

1. Write A then B in both rows. The before ratio is 2:3, the after ratio is 1:2, and both changes are given.
2. Let u be one before-ratio unit. Initially A = 2u and B = 3u; we have not assumed an after-ratio unit.
3. After the changes, A = 2u + 5 and B = 3u + 16. Both quantities have changed from their original values.
4. Because the new B is twice the new A, 2(2u + 5) = 3u + 16. Solve to obtain u = 6.
5. Before A = 12 and B = 18. After A = 17 and B = 34. Both stated changes and both ratios are satisfied.

**Representation:** ratio_table — Use distinct before and after table rows so the before unit 6 is not mistaken for the after unit 17.

**Misconception response:** Compare actual amounts in both rows: A and B increase by different amounts, and the new ratio unit equals 17.

#### Variant B

1. Write before A = 2u and B = 3u, after A = v and B = 2v, keeping the rows separate.
2. The changes link the distinct rows: 2u + 5 = v and 3u + 16 = 2v.
3. Substituting gives 3u + 16 = 2(2u + 5). Hence u = 6, and the before pair is (12,18).
4. After the changes the pair becomes (17,34), which is 1:2; before (12,18) is 2:3.

**Representation:** ratio_table — Separate before/after ratio rows, joined by the absolute changes

**Misconception response:** New ratio units need not equal the original ones; changes must be applied to amounts, not ratio labels.

**Comparison review questions:** Which version makes the central concept visible? Does the extra representation prevent a likely error, or add unnecessary cognitive load? Is the alternative still mathematically sound for the same learner?

### Possible advantages *and* drawbacks (hypotheses, not approvals)

**SPACING_VALID_MAIN:** The specialist number line may be more concrete for P3; a concise subgoal table may be quicker once interval counting is understood. **Potential cost:** Specialist requires an explicit drawing for a gap calculation a fluent learner may already understand.

**SPACING_VALID_ADJACENT:** Both methods may teach this adjacent case equally well. The specialist may provide a visual introduction; the generic approach is sufficiently short. **Potential cost:** Over-teaching a single interval may add unnecessary steps.

**CHAIN_VALID_HALF:** Bar units may lower the burden of fraction equations for P5; symbolic subgoals can be efficient when fraction fluency is established. **Potential cost:** The common-part bar diagram adds a visual conversion step even for algebra-fluent learners.

**CHAIN_VALID_SHARED_BASE:** For simple integer multipliers the incumbent table may be just as teachable; bar models may help children who have not consolidated the shared unit. **Potential cost:** A full three-bar drawing may repeat information already clear in a 1:2:3 table.

**RATIO_VALID_MAIN:** Explicit before/after tables may foreground the changes; ratio bars may support visual learners who understand independent unit sizes. **Potential cost:** The generic ratio-unit scaffold needs additional equations outside its original four teaching stages to solve the change problem.

**RATIO_VALID_DIFFERENT_SCALE:** The specialist makes state transitions explicit; a well-labelled generic ratio table can express the same mathematics without a new method. **Potential cost:** The specialist may be redundant where the ordinary ratio table is already clear and complete.
